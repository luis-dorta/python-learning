Lean. On Friday, November 20, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge released what he says is a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, found with Claude and written as code in Lean, a language that lets computers check every step of a proof. The hypothesis was posed in 1859 and is about the hidden pattern in prime numbers. Scientific American reports that his statement also claims the stronger "generalized" version, and that experts it spoke with saw no immediate real-world use, since it will not change banking or speed up the internet.

On Saturday, the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, the groups behind Lean and its main math library, said they have "full confidence" it is correct. Their checks: dozens of people rebuilt the library and got the same proof file, two separate tools confirmed that the theorem proved matches Mathlib's own wording of the hypothesis, and several independent proof checkers accepted the exported proof. Two limits remain. Alpöge still plans a write-up people can read, and CryptoSlate says the Clay Mathematics Institute still listed the problem as unsolved on November 22. Its prize rules require publication, two years, and broad acceptance. That is our summary of the reporting.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

The useful lesson is how a huge claim earns trust: not by the announcement, but by strangers reproducing it with their own tools. For marketing work, that habit is gold. When a stat or a result looks too good, ask who else checked it and how. When you share AI-assisted work with a client or manager, saying "AI drafted it, and here is how we verified it" is a stronger story than "AI did it." This is our reading, not an expert's.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY

Pick one AI-written fact from a recent campaign brief or report, such as a market stat. Find a second independent source for it, then write "checked against" and the source next to it.

Go deeper

  • A rival claim surfaced the same day. Per Scientific American, a Stanford mathematician may have a human-made proof. A mathematician told the magazine it is "undergoing major revision," and no one has verified it.

  • An MIT mathematician reported that a clean rebuild with networking off matched the published proof file byte for byte, and that two independently written checkers accepted it. CryptoSlate says some of the extra runs were still going on November 22.

  • A University of Waterloo mathematician posted a guide to parts of the argument, but it still leans on the Lean code for a central step, per CryptoSlate.

  • Disclosure: Alpöge works at Anthropic, which makes Claude, and this brief is written with Claude, so weigh our framing with that in mind. We read the main sources below in full.

Sources: Scientific American (news report), Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative joint statement (primary source), CryptoSlate (news report), Scientific American, rival claim

STORY 2 | OPENAI

OpenAI's chief scientist proposes shared limits for AI labs, and shows his own numbers

OpenAI. On Sunday, November 22, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published an essay called "Perplexity and Intelligence." He argues that labs should track perplexity, a score for how well a model predicts the next word piece of text it has never seen. Lower is better. He then proposes "common limits": capability levels that labs agree not to cross until they reach a concrete milestone in understanding and monitoring AI, for example a successor to tools that read a model's reasoning.

As a first step he published OpenAI's own scores, measured on an internal code dataset. GPT-3.5 sat near 0.99, and GPT-4 near 0.91. He says transformative abilities in cybersecurity and math appeared once scores fell below 0.8, and that math results began at about 0.76. He expects the next window, around 0.68 to 0.7, to bring more abilities, though he says he does not know which. This is a proposal, not an agreement, and every number is OpenAI's own. He lists the metric's weak spots himself, including that labs use different datasets, so scores are hard to compare.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

This is really a story about picking a shared metric. If every lab measures differently, nobody can compare. Marketing has the same problem: one channel's "conversion" is not another's. Being able to say what a metric shows, what it hides, and why you chose it is a skill clients and managers notice. Pachocki even lists his own metric's weak points, which is a habit worth copying. This is our reading of one company's essay.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY

Take one metric from a past campaign, such as engagement rate. Write one sentence on what it tells you and one on what it hides. Add a third naming a second metric that would fill the gap.

Go deeper

  • Pachocki admits that publishing the scores gives competitors a clearer picture of where OpenAI stands, but argues the need for shared safety measures outweighs that.

  • Three more weak spots he names: perplexity does not match any single task people care about, reasoning step by step boosts ability without lowering the score, and nobody knows how low the score can go.

  • He says AI help on research into how models are first trained could trigger a burst of advances "as soon as 2027." A footnote credits the steep 2026 drop mostly to long-term research and computing investment, and only partly to automation.

  • This follows his September 6 essay "An Alien Mind," which said no lab had solved alignment and monitoring well enough to keep scaling at full speed "for much longer," per OpenAI's site and The Next Web.

  • We found one primary source for the new post and no independent coverage yet. The post links to an essay on Dario Amodei's website about pacing AI progress. We did not read it.

Sources: OpenAI, Perplexity and Intelligence (primary source, vendor-reported), OpenAI, An Alien Mind (primary source), The Next Web (background on the September essay)

STORY 3 | NICALIS

A hit game pulls AI music after players read the file metadata

Nicalis. The Binding of Isaac: Repentance+ launched an update on November 19 with new platforms. One day later, a Reddit user pointed out tracks in a challenge run themed after GTA 6 whose file metadata named Suno, an AI music tool, and linked to the tracks' pages on Suno's site. Series creator Edmund McMillen wrote on X that he "had no idea" the run used generated music. Publisher Nicalis shipped a patch confirming the "removal of generated music files," live on PC and submitted to Microsoft, Nintendo and Sony, with no console dates yet.

The backlash was fast. IGN, as relayed by GamesHub, says only 8% of recent Steam reviews were positive. Valve requires developers to disclose how they use AI, and both GamesHub and ShiftDelete.Net report that the game's Steam page carried no such note. Nicalis has not explained how the music got into the build. We read GamesHub and ShiftDelete.Net in full, and both cite IGN, which we could not open directly.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

Two lessons for marketing. First, trust breaks when people feel surprised, not just when AI is used. Telling audiences up front is cheaper than apologizing later. Second, even the creator did not know what was in his own product, so know where every asset in your work came from. That includes anything a vendor, template or teammate gave you. This is our reading of the reports.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY

Pick three images, audio clips or text blocks from a recent campaign or social post. Write one line each: who made it, what tool, and whether AI was involved. Add a short "How this was made" note anywhere AI helped.

Go deeper

  • GamesHub says much of the negative review traffic cites the unannounced AI material, and that the episode shows how generated content can reach a shipped game without even its creator knowing.

  • The update also brought the expansion to Nintendo Switch 2, PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X and S, so the timing hurt more.

  • One reviewer argued that adding AI to a game people already paid for should allow refunds, per ShiftDelete.Net. That is one opinion, not a policy.

  • Our cross-check: two outlets agree on the dates, the Suno metadata, McMillen's reply and the patch. Neither says why the music was added.

Sources: GamesHub (news report, read in full), ShiftDelete.Net (news report, read in full, citing IGN)

Tip: critique a post before it reaches your scheduler

In any AI chat tool, ask: "Here is my draft LinkedIn post: [paste it]. Round 1: list the 3 biggest problems and quote the words. Do not rewrite yet. After I pick, rewrite only those parts. Do not post or schedule anything."

How you might use it: if your AI tool can link to a social scheduling app, ask it to save the result as a draft only, so nothing goes public without you. Otherwise, paste the final version into any scheduler yourself. Check the tool's own setup page for steps.

Sources: A tip with no outside claim.

Anthropic's prompting guide says "The first prompt rarely works perfectly," and lists iterating among the mistakes to avoid skipping. Its prompt chaining example has the AI draft, review that draft, then improve it. The round-by-round wording below is our own adaptation, and it works in any AI chat tool.

WEAK

Make my campaign brief better.

STRONG. COPY AND PASTE THIS

I want to improve this: [Paste your draft, for example a campaign brief paragraph or a social post]

Goal: [What it must achieve, for example: get the client to approve the campaign]

Reader: [Who will read it]

Round 1. Do not rewrite anything yet. Give me the 3 biggest problems that hurt the goal, ranked from most to least important. For each one, quote the exact words it comes from and explain the problem in one sentence. Then stop and wait for me.

Round 2. After I reply with the problems I want fixed and any notes, rewrite only those parts. Keep every other word exactly as it is. When you finish, list each change in one line.

Round 3. Ask me whether we should do another round, and name the one thing you would fix next.

Why the strong one works

  • Problems before fixes. Asking for a ranked list first shows you what is wrong, so you do not get a full rewrite you never asked for.

  • You steer. You choose which problems to fix. The AI works for your judgment, not the other way around.

  • Nothing good gets lost. "Keep every other word exactly as it is" protects the parts you already like.

  • Evidence, not vibes. Quoting the exact words ties each comment to something you can see and check.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY. 5 MINUTES

Take one paragraph from a campaign brief or a social post. Run Round 1, pick one problem, then run Round 2. Compare the new paragraph with the old one and decide which words you keep. About 5 minutes.

Sources: Anthropic, Prompt engineering best practices (the "Common mistakes to avoid" list and the prompt chaining example). The three-round structure, the quote requirement and the keep-everything-else line are our own adaptation.
