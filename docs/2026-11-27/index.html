Stanford. On November 25, a Stanford physicist posted a preprint proposing a way to describe gravity with quantum rules, a puzzle physicists have chased for decades. His trick is to stop treating space as a fixed stage. He marks off a region using the paths light could take between two events, a shape called a causal diamond, and does the quantum math inside it. In the paper's acknowledgements, he says several calculations and proofs came from months of back and forth with OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, about 400 million tokens, with OpenAI supplying the computing power.

The paper claims two checks. A calculation that blows up to infinity under a common shortcut gives a finite answer when done his way, and it reproduces a known quantum correction to black hole entropy that matches an earlier result from physicist Ashoke Sen. Now the cautions. It has one author, it has not been peer reviewed, it covers empty space only with no matter, and it offers no experiment to test it. The paper does not say which steps the AI produced. MLQ says no outside physicist had published an assessment by Thursday afternoon.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

The useful habit here is the disclosure. The author told readers exactly how AI was used and how much. When you use AI on a campaign report, a client deck or a batch of social posts, a short note on what it did and what you checked yourself builds trust. That is our reading.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY

Pick one thing you made recently with AI help, such as a campaign brief or a social post. Write a two line "AI use note": what the AI did, and what you verified yourself.

Go deeper

  • The Quantum Insider explains the box with a picture: a flash goes off, a detector records light later, and light paths from each end enclose the region where a signal could travel.

  • Per MLQ, the paper also argues that a diamond of bounded size can hold only a finite number of distinguishable quantum states, and recovers the standard black hole entropy formula for the leading term.

  • Next steps named by The Quantum Insider: extend the theory to include matter, and check whether different ways of building the quantum states give the same theory.

Sources: MLQ (news report, cites the paper), The Quantum Insider (news report), the preprint on arXiv (primary source, linked from both reports; we read it only through their summaries)

STORY 2 | SALESFORCE

AI shopping agents get their first big Black Friday test

Salesforce. Salesforce forecasts that this year's Cyber Week, November 24 to 30, will bring $357 billion in global online sales, and that $75.8 billion of it, 21%, will be "influenced" by AI and agents. It also says about one in five shoppers are using AI. Adobe, which tracks US online shopping, forecast earlier that traffic reaching US retail sites from AI tools will rise 95% year over year on Black Friday and 130% across the whole holiday season. Adobe also expects about $12.9 billion in US online spending today. Both are company forecasts, and both companies sell products to retailers.

Fortune's report this morning adds a reality check. It says 54% of adults have used AI for at least one shopping task, but only 23% trust generative AI to handle payments for them. An American Express survey it cites found 52% are fine with an agent buying cheap household items but only 15% for big purchases, and a Zeta poll found just 9% would let AI buy gifts without checking first. A Bain analysis of Similarweb data says AI assistants make up under 1% of retailers' web traffic. Notice the word "influenced." It means AI played a part, such as research or comparison. It does not mean agents completed the purchase.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

If assistants shop for people, brands have to be easy for an AI to read: clear product details, honest reviews, plain answers. For your own reporting, be careful with metrics that say "influenced." Ask what counts. That is our reading.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY

Ask any AI chat tool to recommend three products for something you plan to buy. Write down which brand details it used and one thing it got wrong or left out.

Go deeper

  • Inc. reports the National Retail Federation says 59% of shoppers plan to use AI to help them buy, and that $76 billion is real money but far from the majority of the roughly $357 billion total.

  • Fortune also describes a test where each shopping agent made 15 trips and only 57% led to a purchase that matched the shopper's needs. Agents often did not ask follow up questions, such as whether a TV works with games.

  • eMarketer found AI-referred traffic to US retail sites rose 393% in the first quarter of 2026, from what Fortune calls an admittedly small base.

Sources: Salesforce (primary source, vendor-reported forecast), Adobe (primary source, vendor-reported forecast), Fortune (news report), Inc. (news report, excerpt only)

STORY 3 | NVIDIA

Nvidia pauses AI server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries

Nvidia. The Financial Times reported on Thursday that Nvidia has told manufacturing partners such as Dell and Supermicro to stop shipping servers that contain its chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. Three people with knowledge of the matter said the partners have told buyers in those countries that their orders will not ship. It is not clear whether the pause is temporary. It follows tighter US export controls meant to stop Chinese companies from renting AI computing power through foreign cloud providers. According to The Decoder, cloud providers in the region now have 90 days to apply for a license, and one executive called the situation "chaos."

The money at stake is large. The FT says Nvidia booked $104 billion in revenue through its Singapore unit in 2025, up 72% in a year, though some Taiwan and China sales are also recorded there. Banks have reportedly paused or slowed financing. The FT, as relayed by The Decoder, says about $500 million in loans to Singapore cloud company Aolani are on hold, while Bloomberg, as relayed by CoinDesk, puts that loan at $480 million and says lenders are taking longer on a separate $6.6 billion loan for Bridge Data Centres in Malaysia. We did not read either original. CoinDesk adds that Nvidia shares have fallen as much as 50% from their October peak.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

AI is not only software. Chips, cloud capacity and government rules decide who can use the tools, and that can affect prices and limits down the line. For marketers, knowing how the AI supply chain works helps you read tech news with a cool head. That is our reading.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY

Pick one brand you follow. Write two lines on how chip supply, cloud costs or export rules could affect its AI plans.

Go deeper

  • The Decoder says US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told CNBC the goal is to block indirect access, including smuggling through Southeast Asia and remote access through data centers in third countries.

  • The Decoder, citing CNBC, says dealers have listed an unusually large number of Nvidia GPUs for sale, including thousands of B300 chips originally meant for data centers with Chinese rental customers.

  • CoinDesk says debt-funded AI infrastructure companies such as CoreWeave, IREN and Cipher Digital have dropped as much as 70% from their highs. If lenders tighten beyond Southeast Asia, it warns, chip orders could slow even while demand for computing stays strong.

  • Reuters's story is a relay of the FT report. Treat the pause as reported, not confirmed by Nvidia in anything we read.

Sources: Reuters (relays the FT, excerpt only), Financial Times (original report, excerpt only), The Decoder (news report), CoinDesk (news report)

DeepL's Aure aims to carry a speaker's tone into translated speech

DeepL | Aure | Voice

DeepL, a translation company, announced Aure, a voice model for live translation. Instead of reading out flat text, it tries to pass the speaker's emotion, emphasis and pace into the translated voice. A Japanese-language write-up of DeepL's November 11 announcement says it covers English, German, Spanish, French, Italian, Japanese, Portuguese and Korean to start, and works in DeepL Voice for online meetings, for in-person conversations, and through an API. The write-up says the announcement had no numbers on accuracy or delay.

Who can use it: mainly businesses that already use DeepL Voice. Good for: meetings and customer calls with people who speak another language. Pricing was not in what we read, so check DeepL's own site.

Claude | Connectors | Beta | Nov 24

Anthropic's release notes say Claude, its AI assistant, can now send a file to an app a user has connected, or save a file that the app offers. In its research guidance, Anthropic suggests developing several competing hypotheses while tracking confidence levels, and critiquing your own approach along the way. Turning that into a prompt for everyday "why did this happen?" questions is our own adaptation.

WEAK

Why did my post get so few likes? Here are the numbers: [pasted numbers]

STRONG. COPY AND PASTE THIS

I want to understand why this happened:

[Describe what happened, for example: my last 5 posts got far fewer likes than usual]

Here are the facts I know:

[Paste numbers, dates, what changed, and anything else you know]

Please do this in order:

1. List 3 or 4 different possible explanations. Do not pick one yet.

2. For each one, name the facts above that support it, the facts that argue against it, and your confidence: low, medium or high.

3. Tell me the cheapest test I could run to tell the top two apart.

4. Mark anything you are assuming instead of reading from my facts.

If my facts are not enough to decide, say so.

Why the strong one works

  • Many answers first. It stops the AI from grabbing the first explanation that sounds good and building a story around it.

  • Evidence both ways. Asking for facts that support and facts that argue against each idea forces it to point at your real data.

  • Confidence labels. Low, medium and high tell you what to trust and what to double check.

  • A next step. The cheapest test turns a nice analysis into something you can do this week.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY. 5 MINUTES

Take one real "why" question from your marketing work, such as why a campaign or a social post underperformed. Fill in the brackets, run the prompt, then run a one line version of the same question. Which answer gave you something you could test? About 5 minutes.

Sources: Anthropic, Prompting best practices (the "Research and information gathering" section). The wording, numbered steps and examples are our own adaptation.

Question 1. Why did the Claude-assisted Riemann hypothesis proof not win the prize right after the Lean check?

  • A. Lean only checks spelling, not logic

  • B. A computer check can confirm the steps, but the prize rules also require publication and a waiting period

  • C. A person, not an AI, has to type the final proof

Question 2. Why call OpenAI's 9/4 matrix result a claim for now?

  • A. It is an unreviewed preprint and only part is machine-checked, so outside experts still need to confirm it

  • B. Because 9/4 is too small a number to matter

  • C. Because it describes an app you can download

Question 3. Salesforce forecasts $75.8 billion of Cyber Week sales will be "influenced" by AI and agents. Which reading is wrong?

  • A. It is a forecast from a company that sells to retailers

  • B. AI agents themselves bought $75.8 billion of goods

  • C. Shoppers may have used AI only to research or compare

ANSWERS. NO PEEKING

1: B. Verification has layers: machine checks, then publication and time for experts to respond.

2: A. Always ask what was verified and by whom. This one is unreviewed, only partly machine-checked, and a theory result.

3: B. Influenced means AI played a part, such as research or comparison. It does not mean agents completed the purchases.
