STORY 2 | SECURITY

AI makes attackers faster, so Anthropic shortens its disclosure clock

Security. Google's threat intelligence group published a report on Monday saying attackers are now using open-source multi-agent frameworks, including tools called ARTEX, CAIRN, CyberStrikeAI and PentAGI. Google says it saw CAIRN and PentAGI used in real activity, and that attackers use AI models to study a new software patch and quickly turn it into an exploit. The report's own point is that the basics have not changed: stolen passwords and unpatched systems are still the way in. Only the speed and scale are different.

Anthropic reacted the same day with a new vulnerability disclosure policy. For reports made on or after January 1, 2027, the window before technical details go public drops from 90 days to 30 for high and critical bugs, and to 45 days for the rest. A researcher can escalate to a coordinator after 15 days instead of 30. The 7-day window for bugs already being exploited stays. Anthropic's stated reason is that AI makes it more likely that two people find the same flaw independently.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

If attackers can turn a patch into an exploit quickly, the safest habit for any brand that runs a website is to update fast. Outdated plugins and reused passwords are the likely weak spots, not exotic AI tricks. For marketers who own a site, a landing page tool or a store, routine maintenance is now part of protecting the brand. That is our reading.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY

Pick one website your team manages and write a five-line maintenance checklist: update the platform and plugins, remove unused plugins, turn on two-step login, check admin users, and save a backup. Put a monthly reminder on your calendar.

Go deeper

  • Google also describes AI-model account resale, sometimes called LLMjacking, and AI use in influence operations.

  • Anthropic's new policy applies only to reports it makes, and only from January 1, 2027. Other companies set their own timelines.

  • Anthropic says it will wait 30 days after a patch before sharing technical details, down from 45.

  • It also points to free programs for open-source maintainers. We did not sign up for or test any of them.

  • Google's report is a vendor's view of threats it observed. It is useful, but it is not an independent count.

Sources: Google Threat Intelligence (primary source, vendor-reported), Anthropic (primary source, company policy)

STORY 3 | WASHINGTON

The U.S. tightens AI chip rules to stop remote access from China

Washington. The Commerce Department issued an interim rule on Monday that requires licenses to export AI chips and servers to data centers anywhere in the world that could provide computing power to companies in China or other restricted countries. TechCrunch says it applies to data centers and networks above a size threshold of about 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips, and it also covers indirect access through brokers. The goal is to close a gap: renting computing time abroad, rather than buying chips, was a way around export limits.

The rule also adds new licensing for chip exports to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, except for chips headed to U.S.-based companies. Existing operators get 90 days to apply for licenses. Commercial recommendation systems, such as those used by online stores and social media, are exempt. TechCrunch adds that it replaces the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule. We found this in one outlet's reporting, so read the rule text before relying on the specifics.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU

The rules around who can use the most powerful AI chips now reach cloud rentals, not just hardware sales. For marketers, the practical effect is indirect: AI tool prices and availability can shift when a supplier faces new compliance work. It is also a live example of policy that marketing and sales teams at AI companies will have to explain to customers. That is our interpretation.

CLIP AND TRY TODAY

Write a plain-language summary of this rule in two sentences, for a customer who has never heard of chip exports. If you cannot explain why remote access matters, reread the second paragraph above.

Go deeper

  • The size threshold is given as 12 million in total processing performance, which TechCrunch converts to roughly 750 high-end Nvidia chips. The conversion is TechCrunch's.

  • This follows weeks of coverage about chip smuggling and Nvidia's leadership change. We have no evidence the rule is tied to any one company.

  • An interim rule can change after public comment, so details may move.

Sources: TechCrunch (one outlet read, news report)

Qwen4-Max is out of preview, and Alibaba says it costs far less per task

Alibaba | Update | Released Nov 15

Yesterday's issue covered Qwen4-Max-Preview. What changed: the full model is now out of preview. Alibaba's blog says it has 2.4 trillion total parameters, with 71 billion active at a time, a 1 million token context window and built-in image understanding. It reports that on its Work360 benchmark the cost per task fell from $4.43 to $1.67, a 62% drop. It also says token use fell by roughly a quarter to a third on Work360 and by almost half on Terminal-Bench 4.0.

Where to get it: QwenCloud (by token plan or API), QwenWork, Qoder and QwenStudio. Alibaba says smaller Qwen4 models will be released as open source. The catch: every number above is Alibaba's own, we found no per-token prices and no outside benchmarks, so treat the savings as a claim. Good for: knowing what a competitor to Claude and ChatGPT claims. We have not tested it.

Sources: Qwen blog (primary source, vendor-reported)

Ask Maps plans trips and can call local businesses

Google | Maps | Launched Nov 16

Google says Ask Maps, the AI assistant inside Google Maps, can now build day-by-day itineraries, which you can export to Google Docs on desktop. It is also adding AI calling that contacts local businesses to book appointments such as beauty, pet care and auto or home repair, starting in the U.S. on Android and iOS. Ask Maps is also reaching 30 more European countries.

Good for: holiday trip planning. For marketers who work with local businesses, note that AI tools may now judge a business by what its listing and website say, so clear hours and services matter.

Sources: Google blog (primary source, vendor-reported)
